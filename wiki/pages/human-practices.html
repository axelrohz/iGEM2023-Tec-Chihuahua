Our beginnings

Everything starts with a necessity, only initiative is required to make a change. Since the beginning of our project, we had our sights set on working with cows, these beautiful animals that provide us with milk, a highly nutritious food for many.1 While investigating current issues in our community, we came across mastitis a bacterial infection that results in swelling and pain, affecting the cow's udder.Dairy mastitis is a significant concern,as it leads to decreased milk production,changes in milk quality, andeconomic losses for dairy farmers. ,a highly common infection in dairy cows. This is when our journey begins.

Aureobos a non-antibiotic treatment for mastitis, strives to minimize the necessity for milk withdrawal during treatment, In this way, food waste and economic losses suffered by milk producers would be reduced. Our goal is to address the challenges of today’s world, which encompass nutritional, economic, and health-related issues. We also aim to contribute to the SDG (Sustainable development goals) number 12 Responsible Consumption and Production, since it contributes to reducing food waste.2 Finally SDG number 15, Life on land3 the use of antibiotics during mastitis treatment is not only eliminated through milk, but also through urine and excrement, with Aureobos we would be reducing land contamination, as endolysins do not have any negative or harmful effect on the soil4, cooperating with the objective of this SDG.

During our visit to one of the Zaragoza industries in Delicias, one of the biggest industry in the country with more than 65 years in the market, we had the opportunity to see a newborn calf. Food waste


Mastitis causes significant milk wastage due to the current treatments, most of which involve antibiotics. The presence of antibiotics leads to a milk withdrawal period, during which the produced milk is discarded because it is not safe for human consumption.16 According to interviews conducted, a cow typically produces 20-35 liters of milk per day. The milk withdrawal period lasts for 5 days, resulting in the loss of 100-175 liters of wasted milk. This represents a very big concern, since in addition to generating economic losses, it contributes to food waste.

Story Behind

From the collection center LICONSA in Delicias, we learned that somatic cells are an important component present in milk and in the udder that indicates health and well-being for the cow. However high SCC (Somatic Cell Count) is associated with, an alteration in milk composition, bacteriological problems in milk, and modifications of dairy products characteristics. Therefore the higher the somatic cell count in the milk, the lower the payment to the producers since this indicates a lower quality of the product.



There is a variety of detection methods used for SCC (Somatic Cell Count), during the interviews with our stakeholders they mentioned three really important methods, California Test, Wisconsin Test, and milk conductivity.

This test is the most commonly used in the industry for subclinical mastitis detection. How to interpret results from the test?

The results can be categorized ranging from a negative outcome where the milk and reagent maintain a liquid consistency to the highest cell count, where the milk-reagent mixture nearly solidifies, so the higher the cell presence, the more DNA concentration is released, leading to increased gelatin formation, ultimately resulting in our interpretation of the result as indicating the most pronounced level of inflammation.

Wisconsin Test

We also had an interview with Gabriela Mapes, a stakeholder in the pharmaceutical area, as she works with Zoetis a well-known global animal health company that specializes in the development and manufacturing of veterinary pharmaceuticals, vaccines, diagnostics, and various other products and services related to animal health and well-being. During our interview, she mentioned the use of WMT test for SCC, so we delved into finding out what it consisted.



The Wisconsin Mastitis Test (WMT) was created with laboratories in mind, and it's like a detective tool for milk quality. It helps us figure out the amount of somatic cells present in fresh blended milk, milk from chilling tanks, and even milk from individual cows. It is similar to the California Test (CMT) in terms of the solution it uses. But here's the cool part: instead of just giving a qualitative result like the CMT, the WMT gives us a quantitative result, that is detailed on the numbers based on how thick the milk becomes. It's like having a precise measurement to understand the milk's health.WMT consists of using a millimeter-marked tube into which you place 2 ml of milk, along with a mixture of 2 ml of CMT reagent and distilled water in equal parts, which is then mixed.

The results are linked to the milliliter markings on the tube and what they tell us about somatic cells. We've got this handy chart that helps us understand what it all means. So, if a herd scores low, for example between 3 and 12, it's doing pretty well—no major worries. But if the score is above 12, it signifies a great concern, and we've got to jump into action right away.19

Milk conductivity

Measurement of electrical conductivity (EC) is a method frequently used in dairy cows during milking in milking parlors, but especially in robotic milking as a low-cost mastitis detection method. Therefore conductivity of milk seems to hold promise as an indicator of subclinical mastitis.20

As any scientific emerging project, there is potential for it to be misused or generate unintended consequences. While we aim to develop AureoBos as an alternative treatment for bovine mastitis in a responsible and beneficial way, it is important to consider certain risks in order to plan how to manage them. Inside the lab, while developing our project, the team has received proper training in order to work in a safe and responsible environment, following a series of measures and guidelines further explained in the safety section. On the other hand, some of the risks we visualize outside the lab are; the possible unregulated use of AureoBos which, without proper guidance, could lead to ineffective treatment or unintended side effects; the overreliance on it, which is important to remember that different cases of mastitis may require different approaches, and a balanced approach to treatment is necessary; an unauthorized distribution that could lead to uncontrolled use and potential safety issues; and, an environmental negative impact where AureoBos is not disposed correctly. Each of these risks, as well as some other legal considerations that directly impact the development of our product, is further detailed and addressed in our legal strategy which you can find on our entrepreneurship section.

In iGEM, 'closing the loop' means teams approach complex biological challenges in a smart and sustainable way. It's not just about making cool biological innovations, but also thinking about how it'll affect the world in the long run – things like ethics, the environment, and society.
So, when we talk about 'closing the loop' for Aureobos, we're talking about making sure our research is responsible and has real-world uses. For three years Ruben worked for Bayer Mexico in the animal health area. He has worked in postpartum management of the cow and breeding. His focus is mastitis and the study of the disorders that cause this disease.

 

Ruben states that malnutrition occurs due to mastitis, a condition that leads to the disposal of numerous liters of milk because of antibiotic presence or elevated somatic cell counts. This reduces the milk supply. Thankfully, the presence of experts, essentially "doctors for the cows," in the barns helps identify these issues. Without their intervention, even more milk would go to waste. Milk containing coliforms can lead to stomach-related illnesses in humans. However, all of this can be prevented by ensuring proper pasteurization.

 

The milking routine begins when the cows are brought from the pens to the milking parlor by the herdsman. It is essential that the herdsman handles them with care, as stress can lead to various illnesses due to immunosuppression, making any bacteria capable of causing a disease. Therefore, rough or stressful treatment by the herdsman while moving the cows can compromise the milking routine. Then, in the holding areas, the cows cool down and thermal stress is reduced before entering the rotary milking system, which is the most common milking system in the Laguna region. Then, he describes the five-step milking protocol which includes: pre-seal application, foremilking, teat drying, attachment of the cluster unit that performs the milking, and post-milking teat seal.

 

To ensure that milk from cows with mastitis is safe for consumption and doesn't pose a risk to public health, certain steps are taken. These steps include:

 

·   Maintaining Clean Teat Cup Liners and Cold Tanks: Regularly cleaning the equipment used to milk the cows, such as the teat cup liners and cold storage tanks.

 

·   Thorough Cleaning After Milking: Implementing a proper and thorough cleaning routine after each milking session.

 

·   Keeping an appropriate temperature in the cold tank: Within the range of 2 to 4 degrees Celsius, to prevent proliferation in milk

 

·   Tank Sampling: Collecting samples from the milk storage tank every time it's emptied into the pipe that leads to the pasteurizer.

 

Monitoring somatic cell counts is also important:

 

·   Higher Somatic Cell Count, Higher Immunological Threat: A higher somatic cell count in the milk indicates a greater risk of potential immune system challenges.

 

·   Ideal Somatic Cell Range: Somatic cell counts should fall within the range of 200,000 to 250,000. If the count goes above 250,000, it may indicate underlying issues.

 

·   Low Somatic Cell Count: Conversely, a somatic cell count below 60,000 suggests minimal immunological threats or the possibility of an immune system malfunction.

 

 

The easiest and most efficient way to detect mastitis, without having to spend on laboratory tests, is at the fore milking. With this step, abnormalities in the characteristics of milk can be identified.

 

The primary treatment for mastitis is Masodine RTU (a ready-to-use solution for teat dip and spray), which doesn't leave any traces in the milk. When mastitis reaches higher levels, an additional treatment called neomycin is needed, which can be detected in the milk. In such cases, the cow needs to be identified and moved to an area where antibiotics are given and milk is segregated. The separated milk is often given to young calves. This is where antibiotic resistance can develop, as the milk retains antibiotic remnants.

 

At times, individuals without veterinary training diagnose mastitis and, having antibiotics on hand, misuse them to quickly address the issue.

 

Administering treatment through the udder (intramammary) is less intrusive than injections. It's better to keep stress levels low, and administering intramammary treatment is simpler.

 

Barns that encounter significant mastitis issues often lack proper hygiene practices. A lower level of technological advancement in the barn increases the likelihood of mastitis development.

Milking Process
Summary:
The interviews carried out from the milking process section provided us with a greater perspective of how bovine mastitis is caused, its current treatments, and how it affects the cow and the diary life of the producers. They mentioned the primordial aspects of the infection, the detection methods, and the milking process. Mastitis is not only related to food waste but also has other impacts, such as antibiotic resistance, 1 where they are left with no treatment options. Additionally, they experience significant economic losses.2 We also had the opportunity to firsthand observe the causes of mastitis and learn about the milk value chain.
Anticipate:
The knowledge we had was primarily based on research because firsthand information from experts was not readily available. We only knew the basics of mastitis, such as the microorganisms involved, symptoms, and the fact that it is an infection in the cow's udder.
Reflection:
Understanding the initial scenario where the problem develops, its causes, and the direct impacts it has on the primary stakeholders: the dairy producers. Gaining insight into the context in which the problem arises, how they prevent or treat it, their concerns regarding bovine mastitis, and their preferences for a more effective solution. This feedback will also help the formulation, presentation, and development of AureoBos.
Engage:
From the various accounts provided by veterinary zootechnicians, zootechnical engineers, and dairy cattle owners, it is evident that mastitis is a significant challenge in dairy cattle management. The primary microorganisms that cause bovine mastitis are S. aureus, S. agalactiae, S. uberis, and E. coli, with E. coli being one of the main microorganisms during the rainy season. They emphasized the importance of hygiene for prevention, with practices such as maintaining cleanliness during the milking routine, using appropriate milking equipment, and ensuring proper pen conditions.
Some of them also mentioned the recent use of vaccines against S. aureus and E. coli to prevent mastitis. They discussed the methods for detecting the infection, primarily during the milking routine, where the technician or person in charge of the milking process identifies changes in the udder such as swelling, hardening, and even the presence of blood. The appearance of yellowish, lumpy, and thinner milk is also a clear sign of mastitis. Other detection methods like the California test, which is the most commonly used test for early mastitis detection in the field, were mentioned.
The severity of mastitis can lead to sending the cow to the slaughterhouse in extreme cases, especially when dealing with S. aureus infections.3 In the other hand e.coli causes recurrence of mastitis and permanent damage to the udders due to the loss of quarters, preventing the cow from reaching its peak production again.4 The treatment approach varies based on severity, with the use of antibiotics like Enrofloxacin, Ceftiofur, Flunixin, and mastitis-specific products commonly employed. Homeopathic treatments were also considered but had adverse effects on milk quality and were not effective. The use of intramammary or intramuscular methods depends on the extent of the infection, with many preferring the intramammary method for its practicality and ease of use.
All of them expressed great concern about the economic losses they experience due to current treatments, which are expensive and require a milk withdrawal period, directly affecting their income from milk sales. Furthermore, many of the current treatments have shown antimicrobial resistance, rendering them ineffective.1 Different farmers highlighted the impact of mastitis on milk production, emphasizing the need for effective treatments and management practices. Many also expressed their interest and enthusiasm to try AureoBos.
Applied/Implementation:
Based on all the interviews, it is evident that mastitis poses a significant challenge in the management of dairy cattle, resulting in reduced production, milk wastage, and significant economic losses. This underscores the need for effective treatments and management practices. Most of the interviewees mentioned that they prefer the intramammary method due to its practicality in terms of application, they also stated that this method is very specific as it is directly applied to the affected area and is one of the most effective and commonly used methods.6 that´s why we chose this method as the application method for AureoBos. They mentioned that the severity of mastitis can lead to culling or sending the cow to the slaughterhouse in some cases, especially when dealing with S. aureus infections, a microorganism that also causes permanent damage to udders, preventing the cow from ever reaching its peak production again. This is where AureoBos comes in; our product targets S. aureus, S. agalactiae, S. uberis and, even better, does not require a milk withdrawal period. Additionally, we discovered that there is a significant incidence of bovine mastitis caused by E. coli, so we decided to add an endolysin that targets this bacteria.

Collective Impact Explained

Collective Impact is an innovative methodology that allows us to incorporate the notion of all parties involved in a problem to deeply understand the areas of opportunity and thus begin to develop strategies to address a collective solution. The Collective Impact framework consists of five core conditions: the development of a common agenda that is shared among all stakeholders; shared measurement strategies to understand if progress is being made; engage in mutually reinforcing activities; continuous communications and backbone infrastructure support. By having collaborative planning, optimal conditions are promoted to effectively address the problem, during development to measure the change to finally understand the impact.

Common Agenda

To collectively understand the impact of mastitis, we developed this problem tree. It is a graphic representation that allows us to understand that all our stakeholders share the same concern, mastitis, and that different situations arise that affect us to a greater and lesser extent.

Shared Measurement    

Mastitis affects different stakeholders along the value chain in various ways. To represent this, we have chosen to use a traffic light system: the red color indicates those involved who suffer a severe negative impact due to mastitis; the yellow color signals those whom mastitis affects, but not severely; and the green color denotes those affected moderately. Additionally, we propose the establishment of a shared database to register mastitis cases each year. This database will be instrumental in taking measures and assisting heavily affected stables. 

Mutually Reinforcing Activities 

The dairy community in our country gathers annually at DIGAL (an acronym in Spanish for International Dairy Farmer's Day). We propose establishing a dedicated space to discuss matters related to mastitis. This would include preventive measures, current treatments, and alternative treatment options. In addition to this, we generated a manual that includes relevant topics for proper management of dairy pens. The purpose of this is to offer homogeneous information for all dairy farmers.

Continuous Communication

To maintain ongoing communication with all stakeholders, we propose developing a network where ideas promoting both mastitis prevention and treatment can be discussed and shared. The Chihuahua's Milk Network has the following objectives:

  • Research and aid in the development of milk production with lower microbial load.

  • Implement programs to assist small and medium-sized farms.

  • Inform all milk producers about public policies and practices that improve the quality and quantity of milk production.

Backbone Support

The most influential representatives from each stakeholder will be tasked with convening and coordinating monthly meetings, fostering training and facilitating discussions on crucial ideas for mastitis prevention and treatment.

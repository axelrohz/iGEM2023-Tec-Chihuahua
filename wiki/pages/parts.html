Introduction

In the heart of Chihuahua, Mexico, lies our tight-knit team, bound not only by our passion for science but also by a deep love for our community. Each one of us has a unique connection to this land, whether we were born here or have spent most of our lives embracing its breathtaking landscapes and basking in the warmth of its people. This beautiful state thrives on its economy driven by mining, agriculture, and livestock sectors. Their determination and hard work have been the lifeblood of our local economy, shaping the fundamental structure of our society. In the early stages of our project, we pondered the possible challenges to tackle, and one of our teammates, Alexa, shared a story about the issues her godmother, a woman who owns a dairy farm, faced. This sparked our curiosity, and we embarked on an online investigation into the world of dairy farming. During our research, a pressing issue captured our attention – mastitis, a prevalent problem in the dairy industry. We became aware of the significant financial losses that this disease caused for facility owners, a revelation that struck a chord with us, and we felt compelled to explore this issue further. Our idea started with our desire to improve the local dairy industry since Chihuahua was the fourth main milk producer in the country in 2022, making up to 490,274 thousand liters[1] and is currently the third place in our country with the biggest population of dairy cows[2].

Into the Udder: Bovine Mastitis

Udder health is a critical concern for the dairy industry worldwide, and bovine mastitis stands as the most prevalent and costly disease affecting dairy cattle[3]. Characterized by the inflammation of the mammary gland due to microbial infection, this condition has multiple causes, including poor environmental hygiene, suboptimal milking practices, and other management factors[4]. (imagen de ubre con y sin mastitis. Mejor si son de archivo propio.) The impact of bovine mastitis extends beyond individual cows, encompassing three distinct classes of inflammation: clinical, subclinical, and chronic[3]. The clinical form manifests with evident symptoms such as redness and swelling of the udder, accompanied by fever and changes in milk appearance. Subclinical mastitis, however, remains hidden, resulting in decreased milk production and an elevated somatic cell count. Chronic mastitis, a persistent and challenging form of the disease, presents sporadic clinical flare-ups over several months[3]. Figure 1. Levels of bovine mastitis and its signs and symptoms. Among the numerous causal agents, Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus agalactiae are regarded as contagious pathogens contributing to bovine mastitis. S. aureus, the most prevailing pathogen known to cause bovine mastitis, is a profiling milk contaminant that produces harmful toxins and enzymes that damage milking tissue. On the other hand, S. agalactiae is adept at forming biofilms and resides in the bovine gastrointestinal tract. The disease’s occurrence is influenced by various host factors, including age, breeding, genetics, and udder structure[3].

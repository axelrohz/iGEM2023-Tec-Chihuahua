Into the Udder: Bovine Mastitis

Udder health is a critical concern for the dairy industry worldwide, and bovine mastitis stands as the most prevalent and costly disease affecting dairy cattle[3]. Characterized by the inflammation of the mammary gland due to microbial infection, this condition has multiple causes, including poor environmental hygiene, suboptimal milking practices, and other management factors[4]. (imagen de ubre con y sin mastitis. Mejor si son de archivo propio.) The impact of bovine mastitis extends beyond individual cows, encompassing three distinct classes of inflammation: clinical, subclinical, and chronic[3]. The clinical form manifests with evident symptoms such as redness and swelling of the udder, accompanied by fever and changes in milk appearance. Subclinical mastitis, however, remains hidden, resulting in decreased milk production and an elevated somatic cell count. Chronic mastitis, a persistent and challenging form of the disease, presents sporadic clinical flare-ups over several months[3].
Among the numerous causal agents, Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus agalactiae are regarded as contagious pathogens contributing to bovine mastitis. S. aureus, the most prevailing pathogen known to cause bovine mastitis, is a profiling milk contaminant that produces harmful toxins and enzymes that damage milking tissue. On the other hand, S. agalactiae is adept at forming biofilms and resides in the bovine gastrointestinal tract. The disease’s occurrence is influenced by various host factors, including age, breeding, genetics, and udder structure[3].

Beating Mastitis

Mastitis is often treated using various methods including intramammary infusion, dry cow therapy, vaccination, and antibiotics [9]. In Mexico, the government approves the use of a wide spectrum of antibiotics for mastitis treatment. Additionally, alternative treatments like bacteriophages, probiotics, and herbal remedies are available, providing a range of options for dairy farmers. Among the existing treatments, intramammary infusion of antibiotics has shown significant effectiveness in reducing mastitis incidence and improving milk quality. Strict regulations in many countries require a withdrawal period after administering antibiotics to dairy cows. During this time, known as the withdrawal period, milk from the treated cow must not be collected for human consumption, leading to economic losses for the producers[5]. Due to our [human practices] interviews, the monthly incidence of mastitis must be 5%, and most of the antibiotics require a 3 day withdrawal, a cow per day produces an average 25 liters of milk, so in only one month producers would be losing near 375 liters per month.

Milk is Moneyr

The implications of bovine mastitis resound throughout the dairy industry, implying great costs that include treatment, animal rearing, and compromised milk production. Furthermore, the composition, quantity, and quality of the milk are negatively impacted, while affected cows may suffer long-term aftermath, including damaged udders that impede future milk production[4]. For the Mexican dairy industry and economies worldwide, bovine mastitis stands as a leading cause of economic loss[6]. As the number of dairy cattle rises, the incidence of this disease is projected to increase, making the bovine mastitis market size a significant contributor to the global economy by 2027, according to the research published by the University of Glasgow, mastitis is estimated to cost around 19,7 billion dollars to 30 billion USA worldwide, this includes several costs, such as diagnostics, milk loss, veterinary services, labor, therapeutics, among others[7 ].

The Risk Beyond

What if this milk did not go to waste? Due to their efficiency, antibiotics continue to be widely used by cattle owners. Unfortunately, this extensive use results in significant portions of antibiotics being released through milk, posing serious risks to human health[5]. The emergence of antibiotic resistance, a global concern, is a direct consequence of this overuse, affecting both human and animal populations. Antibiotic resistance can be either innate or acquired, with plasmid-induced resistance being a major driver of its spread. Additionally, biofilm growth has been found to protect bacteria from the effects of antibiotic treatment. Consequently, improper administration of antibiotics can lead to counterproductive outcomes, increasing antibiotic resistance[9]. Antimicrobial resistance is something inevitable, but the situation we are facing is the consequence of the disproportionate and mistaken usage of antibiotics. The World Bank estimated that up to 3.8% of the global gross domestic product could be lost due to antimicrobial resistance by 2050[10]. Globally, antimicrobial resistance is recognized as one of the top 10 global health threats. Alarmingly, it is estimated that approximately 4.95 million deaths in 2019 were associated with bacterial antibiotic resistance (GLASS Report). Hence, it is crucial to raise awareness about the implications of antibiotic use in our food supply.

The New Antimicrobial Era

Bacteriophage endolysins are considered a great alternative for antibiotics since they have shown specific antimicrobial activity against gram-positive bacteria, even in antibiotic-resistant strains. Using the power of endolysins, we approach the bovine mastitis problem caused by three gram-positive bacteria: S. aureus, S. uberis, and S. agalactiae. The mechanism of endolysins is very specific: they interact with the bacterial cell membrane, causing cellular lysis. These proteins have two ends: the N-terminal, which is the catalytic domain, and the C-terminal, which is the cell wall binding domain that determines the specificity of the endolysin against bacteria[11]. Nonetheless, the endolysin’s in vivo half-life is around 20 minutes[12]. This exceedingly brief time frame severely limits its ability to act effectively as an active compound drug. It is crucial to emphasize that this 20-minute time span is far from sufficient to encompass the entire process of production, purification, distribution, storage, and other essential steps required for the drug's successful application and availability in the market. This short duration limits its ability to act effectively as an active compound drug. For this reason, we look forward to increasing its half-life by binding the protein to an albumin-binding domain[13].For our solution, we use two endolysins: LysCSA13 and LysK.
